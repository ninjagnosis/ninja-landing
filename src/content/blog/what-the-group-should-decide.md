A company with several products is easy to describe and easy to get wrong. It owns them, and the mistake is to think that owning them means running them.

## The four things only the group can decide

Some decisions cannot be made inside one product, because they depend on what the other products need.

![A historic cream-coloured government building with arched windows in Kathmandu](../../assets/blog/what-the-group-should-decide/samsad.jpg "Some decisions only make sense from above one office. Photo: Krish Dulal, CC BY-SA 3.0")

- **Where capital goes.** Money is limited. Someone has to say that this product gets the next investment and that one does not.
- **Which products exist.** Starting a product and stopping one are group decisions. A product team will rarely argue for its own end.
- **Who leads.** Choosing the people who run each product is the most important appointment the group makes.
- **The rules that must match.** Finance, legal standing, security and brand need one answer across the group.

## What to leave alone

Everything else is better decided close to the customer: what to build this month, how to price it, how to answer a support request. A group that reaches into these decisions slows every product down and teaches its leaders to wait for permission.

![Visitors walking through Kathmandu Durbar Square beside a Nepali flag](../../assets/blog/what-the-group-should-decide/durbar.jpg "Daily decisions belong where the customers are. Photo: Jayen Shrestha, CC BY-SA 4.0")

## A test

For any decision, ask whether the answer changes depending on what another product is doing. If it does, the group decides. If it does not, the product decides. Most decisions fail that test, which is the point: the group should make few decisions, and make them well.
