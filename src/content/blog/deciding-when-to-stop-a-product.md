Starting a product feels like progress. Stopping one feels like failure. A group that cannot stop anything ends up spreading the same people thinly over products that do not work.

## Set the question early

When you start a product, write down what would make you continue and what would make you stop: a number, a date, a customer who says yes. It is much easier to apply a rule you wrote before you were attached.

## Look at the opportunity cost

The question is not whether the product has any value. Most do. The question is whether the same people and money would produce more somewhere else in the group.

![Five colleagues around a table reviewing papers in a meeting](../../assets/blog/deciding-when-to-stop-a-product/staff-meeting.jpg "Decide with the numbers on the table. Photo: Bill Branson (Photographer), Public domain")

## Stop cleanly

- Tell customers early, and tell them what happens to their data.
- Move the people to work that matters, and say so.
- Write down what you learned. A stopped product that teaches the group something has paid some of its cost back.
