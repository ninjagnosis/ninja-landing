The first ten engineers decide what a company becomes. They write the code that everything else sits on, and they set the habits that the next fifty people will copy without knowing they are copying. A weak early hire is not just a weak hire. It is a slower company for years.

In Kathmandu, hiring those first ten has its own shape. The pool of experienced product engineers is smaller than the demand. Many strong people are tempted to leave for work abroad. A company that cannot afford a recruiter, a long process or a famous name still has to find people who are good and willing to stay.

This post sets out how a small group like ours thinks about it: what to ask for, how to test it, and how to keep people once you have them.

## Decide what you are hiring for

Before you write a job post, write down the work. Not a title, but the actual problems the person will own in their first six months. "Build the billing service so a customer can pay by wallet and get a receipt." "Make the call transcriber fast enough that a supervisor sees words as they are spoken."

A list of problems does two things. It tells candidates what the job really is, which attracts people who want that work. And it gives you something to test them against, which a generic list of skills never does.

Be honest in it about what is unglamorous. Much of early engineering is support, migrations and fixing what broke at night. Candidates who learn this after joining leave. Those who knew it in advance and still said yes tend to stay.

## Set the bar before you meet anyone

Hiring bars get lowered one tired afternoon at a time. You have an empty seat, the work is piling up, and a candidate who is almost good enough starts to look good enough.

Write the bar down first, in a few lines, before the first interview. A useful bar has three parts:

- **Can do the work.** They have shipped something real, and can explain how it works and where it breaks.
- **Learns fast.** They have picked up something new and unfamiliar and can describe how.
- **Works well with others.** They disagree without making it personal and ask for help when stuck.

Then keep to it. When you are tempted to waive part of the bar, make yourself say which part and why, out loud, to another person. Most of the time you will not want to say it.

It is also worth remembering that an early team is small enough to be damaged by one person. A brilliant engineer who is hard to work with can cost more than they produce, because the others quietly route around them.

## Hire for learning, not for a tool list

Technology changes. A person who knows one framework deeply but cannot learn another will be stuck in two years. A person who learns quickly will be useful in any of them.

For early hires in particular, look for people who have built things end to end, even small things. Someone who has built a full project on their own, including deployment and the boring parts, understands how pieces fit together in a way that a person who has only worked on one layer often does not.

Degrees and previous employers are weak signals. A project they can show you, and talk about in detail, is a strong one. Ask what they would do differently now. People who have really done the work always have an answer, and it is usually specific.

## Use a paid trial task

Interviews measure how well someone interviews. A trial task measures how they work.

The task should be small, real and paid. A few hours of work on a problem close to what the job will be, with a clear description, access to the people who can answer questions, and a fee for their time. In Kathmandu, where many strong candidates are already employed or freelancing, paying for the time also signals that you respect it.

A few principles keep a trial task fair and useful:

- **Make it self-contained.** It should be possible to finish without internal knowledge.
- **Keep it short.** Half a day is plenty. Longer tasks filter for people with free time, not people with skill.
- **Let them ask questions.** How someone asks tells you more than the code does.
- **Talk about it afterwards.** Walk through the choices together. Ask what they left out and why.

You are not looking for a perfect solution. You are looking for sound judgement, clear code, honest notes about what is unfinished, and how they respond to a question like "why did you do it this way?"

## Hire slowly, one or two at a time

It is tempting to hire in a batch when you find the budget. Resist it.

Every new engineer needs someone to teach them the codebase, the conventions and the reasons behind past decisions. That someone is one of your existing engineers, whose own work stops while they do it. Three people joining in the same month can halve the output of the team for a quarter.

One or two at a time lets each person settle, produce something real and become a teacher for the next. It is slower on paper and faster in practice.

## Pay fairly, and say how you decide

Compensation is not the whole reason people stay, but unfairness is a quick reason to leave. A few points help.

- **Know the market.** Ask people what comparable roles pay. Do not rely on what you paid last year.
- **Be consistent.** Two people doing similar work should not be paid very differently without a reason you can explain.
- **Review on a schedule.** If raises only come when someone threatens to leave, you teach people to threaten.
- **Explain the system.** Even a simple, written description of how pay is decided removes much of the suspicion.

A small group will rarely out-pay the largest employers or foreign remote jobs. It can be honest, fair and predictable, and that matters more than many founders expect.

## Give people something to own

The best reason to join a small company is that you get to own something real. The best reason to leave is that you do not.

Give each engineer a problem or a part of the product that is theirs: they make the decisions, they are the person others ask, and they answer for how it runs. Ownership has a cost, since people will make mistakes. A team that punishes mistakes will find its engineers stop owning anything.

Pair ownership with a path. Engineers who can see what the next, harder piece of work is will stay for it. Those who can see only more of the same will begin to look.

## Retention starts on day one

Most departures are decided in the first few months, not the last week. A new engineer who has no clear first task, no one to ask and no sign that their work matters will start doubting the decision to join.

Plan the first weeks. Pair them with a person, give them a small real task they can finish in a few days and ship it. A shipped change in the first week does a great deal for how a person feels about a job.

Then keep talking. A regular conversation about how the work is going, what they want to learn and what is getting in their way finds problems while they are small. Most people do not announce they are unhappy. They simply begin to leave.

## What this adds up to

Hiring the first ten is not a funnel problem. It is a series of ten careful decisions, each one affecting the ones after it.

Know the work. Write the bar before you meet anyone. Test with real, paid work. Add people one or two at a time. Pay fairly and explain how. Give each person something to own. Then pay attention to them from the first day.

None of that is clever. It is a lot of small, honest, consistent effort, and in a company with ten engineers, it is also most of what the company is.
